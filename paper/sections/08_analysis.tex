\section{Discussion and Conclusion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Our results support a two-stage practice. Compute the oracle gap on the target dataset-model pair; a positive gap licenses repair with a bounded counter-bias such as \rfc, while a non-positive gap predicts harm and calls for decision-time signals such as descriptor-driven abstention. Several natural mechanisms for exploiting future-structure information turn out not to work, including conditioning the forecast on the predicted structure, per-window gating of the repair, and scaling the descriptor objective to foundation-model fine-tuning (Appendix~\ref{app:negative}). Limitations bound the claims: positive repair evidence concentrates on positive-gap datasets, and the descriptor head's volatility output is too weak to use despite oracle rankings showing it carries the largest selective-prediction signal. All experiments are in the point-forecasting regime; extending the diagnostic and repair to distributional forecasting is the natural next step.

Over-smoothing is not one phenomenon but two. Rational shrinkage is the price of honest uncertainty, and unexploited structure is the repairable part. The oracle gap tells them apart cheaply and predicts, without exception in our evaluation, when anti-smoothing pays. Where it pays, a region-weighted loss recovers part of the structure; where it does not, a descriptor head reports when the forecast deserves trust. We hope the diagnostic becomes a standard instrument, and that future benchmarks report not only accuracy but the structure that remains to be exploited.
