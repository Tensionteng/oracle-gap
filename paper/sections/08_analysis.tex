\section{Discussion and Conclusion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{When to repair, when to report.}
Our results support a three-branch decision practice: compute the oracle gap and the residual-asymmetry probe on the target dataset-model pair. A positive gap licenses a bounded counter-bias such as \rfc; systematic level lag that is not context-predictable also licenses it; a context-predictable lag calls for information injection instead ($-13.2\%$ on saugeenday); and residuals that behave as noise mean the model is already at its rational shrinkage, where decision-time signals such as descriptor-driven abstention are the right instrument. The diagnostics are cheap enough to run as a standard preprocessing step.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
Positive repair evidence concentrates on positive-gap or lag-positive pairs, and several natural mechanisms for exploiting future-structure information do not work: conditioning the forecast on predicted structure, per-window gating of the repair, scaling descriptor supervision to foundation-model fine-tuning, and supervising the future mean level (Appendix~\ref{app:negative}). The descriptor's volatility channel is much weaker than its change-point channel, only partially repaired by a continuous log-volatility target on ETTm1. All experiments are in the point-forecasting regime; extending the diagnostic and repair to distributional forecasting is the natural next step.

Over-smoothing is not one phenomenon but two. Rational shrinkage is the price of honest uncertainty, and unexploited structure is the repairable part. The oracle gap tells them apart cheaply and predicts, in all but one of sixteen cells, when anti-smoothing pays---and the exception is diagnosable from the prediction geometry before training. Where it pays, a region-weighted loss recovers part of the structure; where it does not, a descriptor head reports when the forecast deserves trust. We hope the diagnostic becomes a standard instrument, and that future benchmarks report not only accuracy but the structure that remains to be exploited.
