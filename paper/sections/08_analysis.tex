\section{Discussion and Conclusion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{When to repair, when to report.}
Our results support a decision practice with three branches. Compute the oracle gap and the residual-asymmetry probe on the target dataset-model pair; a positive gap licenses a bounded counter-bias such as \rfc, systematic level lag that is not context-predictable also licenses it, and a context-predictable lag instead calls for information injection such as a post-hoc correction ($-13.2\%$ on saugeenday). Where the residuals behave as noise, the model is already at its rational shrinkage, and decision-time signals such as descriptor-driven abstention are the right instrument. The diagnostics are cheap enough to run as a standard preprocessing step.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
Positive repair evidence concentrates on positive-gap or lag-positive datasets, and several natural mechanisms for exploiting future-structure information turn out not to work, including conditioning the forecast on the predicted structure, per-window gating of the repair, scaling the descriptor objective to foundation-model fine-tuning, and supervising the future mean level (Appendix~\ref{app:negative}). The volatility channel of the descriptor head is much weaker than the change-point channel, repaired only partially by a continuous log-volatility target on ETTm1. All experiments are in the point-forecasting regime; extending the diagnostic and repair to distributional forecasting is the natural next step.

Over-smoothing is not one phenomenon but two. Rational shrinkage is the price of honest uncertainty, and unexploited structure is the repairable part. The oracle gap tells them apart cheaply and predicts, without exception in our evaluation, when anti-smoothing pays. Where it pays, a region-weighted loss recovers part of the structure; where it does not, a descriptor head reports when the forecast deserves trust. We hope the diagnostic becomes a standard instrument, and that future benchmarks report not only accuracy but the structure that remains to be exploited.
