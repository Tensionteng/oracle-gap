\section{Introduction}

Deep forecasters trained with mean squared error produce predictions that are visibly smoother than the series they imitate. On ETTh1, a well-tuned PatchTST model predicts with an amplitude ratio of 0.84 relative to the realized series, and half of its point predictions fall strictly between a level reference and the realized value. This over-smoothing signature is widely treated as a defect. Frequency-domain losses attribute it to label autocorrelation \citep{fredf}, auxiliary classification heads attribute it to the failure of squared losses on high-entropy features \citep{hcan}, and shape-aware objectives penalize it directly \citep{dilate}. Underlying these works is a common premise: the smoothness is pathological, and repairing it should improve accuracy.

We show that this premise needs qualification. A squared-loss-optimal forecast is the conditional mean of the future given the context, and the conditional mean is necessarily smoother than any realization, because realizations contain unpredictable noise that no point forecast should imitate. The over-smoothing signature is therefore a property of the optimal predictor itself, not only of imperfect models. Whether a trained model is \emph{too} smooth depends on how its signature compares with the signature an oracle would leave on the same data. This reframing decomposes over-smoothing into \emph{rational shrinkage}, which an optimal predictor must keep, and \emph{unexploited structure}, which can in principle be repaired.

We make the decomposition measurable. Our diagnostic, the \emph{oracle gap}, bootstraps a trained model's residuals to synthesize alternative realizations of the future, computes the amplitude signature the model's predictions would exhibit as an oracle against those realizations, and compares it with the signature observed against the true data. The gap needs no training and runs in seconds per dataset-model cell. A positive gap indicates residuals with structure the model failed to extract; a non-positive gap indicates rational shrinkage.

The diagnostic is predictive, not just descriptive. Across thirteen dataset-backbone cells and a 665-cell audit of thirteen foundation models on fifty GIFT-Eval variants \citep{gifteval}, the sign of the gap predicts the sign of every anti-smoothing intervention we tested, without a contradictory cell. The map also settles several folk questions: the ETT benchmarks are saturated in the sense that even an oracle would look over-smoothed on them, transit and energy series retain exploitable structure across all thirteen foundation models, and the gap is flat across model scale from 8M to 311M parameters in the zero-shot regime.

Guided by the diagnostic, we introduce \rfc, a region-weighted loss that increases the penalty where the prediction under-shoots the realized deviation from a level reference, while leaving the zero-loss solution set of MSE untouched. A single coefficient controls the strength, and a Gaussian analysis bounds the induced fixed-point shift at roughly $0.4\alpha\sigma$, so the loss acts as a calibrated counter-bias rather than a new objective. On Electricity, \rfc~reduces MSE by 2.3\% across three seeds and across horizons from 96 to 720 steps; on datasets with non-positive gap it degrades gracefully to the MSE baseline.

Where shrinkage is rational, a model can still be made more useful by knowing when it is likely to fail. A light-weight descriptor head predicts structural properties of the future window, with labels computed for free from training data. Such heads cannot repair point accuracy at the scales we study, echoing scale-dependent findings for multi-token prediction \citep{gloeckle2024mtp,fsf}, but the predicted change-point probability is a reliable forward-looking risk signal: abstaining on the 20\% highest-risk windows reduces the remaining MSE by up to 5.3\%, outperforming oracle change-point rankings and static volatility baselines. Predicting future structure is more useful for deciding when to trust a forecast than for improving the forecast itself.

\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A two-component theory of over-smoothing separating rational shrinkage from unexploited structure, with a conditional improvement interval $(0, 2b)$ and a Jensen-gap argument for supervising structure through auxiliary heads rather than on the output.
    \item The oracle gap, a training-free diagnostic of repairable structure, validated as a predictor of intervention outcomes across 678 dataset-model cells without contradiction.
    \item \rfc, a region-weighted loss that repairs over-smoothing where the diagnostic is positive and degrades gracefully elsewhere, with a bounded fixed-point shift.
    \item An audit of forecasting benchmarks through repairable structure: saturation evidence for ETT, positive cells in transit and energy series, flat scaling from 8M to 311M parameters, and complementarity between task models and foundation models.
    \item Evidence that predicted structural descriptors are more valuable at decision time than at training time, including a selective-prediction gain of 5.3\% that exceeds oracle change-point ranking.
\end{itemize}
